I recently read the paper [*Why Does Action Chunking Improve Behavioral Cloning Performance in Robotic Control?*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.02547), which makes an interesting observation:

> There is evidence that human's visually guided behavior operates at a frequency in the 2-10Hz range, and, as a result, at a control frequency of 50-60Hz, actions are recorded at a much higher frequency than the human demonstrator is updating their behavior, so there may exist significant temporal correlation between actions that is effectively modeled with action chunking.

This got me thinking about the theory of constraints, and *The Goal* by Eliyahu Goldratt. One of the key tenets of the book is that every system's weakest link or bottleneck is the constraint dictating total output. Applying that idea to robotics learning and inference-based control: the current bottleneck looks like model inference time, which dictates a slow decision rate somewhere between 1 and 10Hz.

Suppose we eliminate that bottleneck. Goldratt predicts a new constraint surfaces somewhere else in the system, and I want to find out where.

The elimination has started. Atharva Gundawar, co-founder of Markov Robotics, [posted a demo](https://x.com/_gundawar/status/2089466504844918961) in August 2026 of real-time chunked world models for dexterous hands: autonomous rollouts playing at 1x speed, with each chunk generated in 200ms. The individual pieces existed already. Game environments have real-time interactive world models (Genie 3, Oasis), and dexterous manipulation has world models trained from human video ([DexWM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.13644)). I hadn't seen the two combined before: a world model generating as fast as real time, with a policy acting inside it. A world model that runs faster than real time can sit inside the control loop, letting the robot evaluate candidate futures before committing to an action.

We'll walk through this thought experiment in two parts:

1. What zero-latency replanning does to a frozen VLA in simulation, plus what the literature says about the regime above 40Hz (this post)
2. Whether we still need a tiered L1/L2 control paradigm in this new world, and the frontiers it could unlock (next week)

## What the evidence says about fast replanning

The experiment I want: take a frozen VLA, run it in simulation, and sweep how often it gets to replan. Simulation matters because the simulator pauses while the policy computes, so inference is free, which is the exact condition this thought experiment assumes. Two groups have run pieces of this comparison (both appear below), one in a scripted state-based setting and one on Push-T with small policies. Nobody has run it on a frozen production-scale VLA, and nobody has run the arm that separates the two competing explanations for what goes wrong.

A literature review before attempting it reveals the following.

1. High-frequency execution is worth having in the first place. [Wang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.24931) open with a replay comparison: the same one-second motion, peaking at 120 mm/s, discretized at 60Hz replays in 1.05 seconds; discretized at 15Hz it takes 5.31 seconds, because the arm brakes toward each sparse position target before starting on the next one. Low-frequency execution turns smooth motion into stop-and-go. (They present this as a motivating figure rather than a documented experiment, so treat the exact numbers as directional. The paper's contribution is a VAE that lets policies emit 60Hz chunks in a latent space; the figure is its motivation.)

2. The paper that opened this essay contains its own frequency observation, reported as a two-sentence aside in the discussion rather than a tabulated experiment. [Lazzati et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.02547) ran their main results at 15-20Hz. At 50-60Hz, they report, single-step policies conditioned on delayed observations stopped matching action chunking, and grouping actions into sub-chunks of five, which brings the effective decision rate back near 10Hz, "significantly improved" performance. Their explanation is the quote at the top: humans update visually guided behavior at 2-10Hz, so a 50-60Hz recording contains five to twenty frames per human decision, and the policy has to model that correlation across steps somehow. The rest of their paper matters for my experiment, and it cuts against the latency framing: their simulated results already run with inference free, and chunking still beats single-step policies by wide margins. The win comes from statistics (non-Markovian expressivity, conditioning on past observations, an implicit ensembling effect), and delayed single-step policies replanning at every step match chunking across LIBERO (94.0 vs 89.2 on Libero-90).

3. [Bidirectional Decoding](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.17355) supplies the cleanest published measurement of zero-latency fast replanning. On deterministic Push-T, a VQ-BeT policy executing its full chunk succeeds 64.0% of the time; the same policy replanning after every action drops to 48.9%. The simulator waits for the model in both cases, so latency explains none of the gap. Each requery draws an independent chunk from a multimodal action distribution, and the policy oscillates between strategies instead of committing to one. Add heavy action noise and the ranking flips: per-step replanning scores 29.5% against 13.0% for the full chunk, and in a separate 1-D diagnostic with transition noise, per-step replanning beat every longer horizon they tried. Which strategy wins depends on how hard the environment pushes back. Their fix is a test-time selection rule: sample several candidate chunks and keep the one most consistent with the unexecuted remainder of the previous one. My experiment borrows this rule.

4. The strongest zero-latency evidence arrived in August 2026, in a [blog post by Seohong Park](https://seohong.me/blog/behavioral-cloning-mystery/). He built a state-based sim benchmark with scripted demonstrations that mimic human data ("very narrowly distributed, highly temporally correlated (non-Markovian), and 'smoothly' random"), 50Hz control, and a data generator fast enough to train on every sample once, an infinite-data regime. A flow-matching policy predicting 25-action chunks solves block pick-and-place. The same policy queried at every step fails outright; in his words, "it doesn't even touch the cube," with infinite data and zero latency. He attributes the gap to compounding error from frequent stochastic queries plus an expressivity mismatch: the demonstrations are non-Markovian, so a per-step policy learns a "Markovianized" behavior the data never contained. Two more of his findings matter here. History conditioning, the obvious consistency fix, made his policies worse. And his 25-step chunks at 50Hz put the effective decision rate at 2Hz, at the bottom edge of the human visuomotor band from item 2.

5. Theory caught up to the practice this year. [Zhang, Pfrommer, Pan, Matni, and Simchowitz](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09061) prove that when the open-loop dynamics are incrementally stable, which end-effector control on top of a low-level tracking controller supplies, executing chunks open-loop converts the exponential compounding of imitation error into a constant-factor bound, and the required chunk length grows only logarithmically in the stability constants. One detail carries weight for this essay: the guarantee comes from executing the chunk open-loop. A policy that predicts chunks but replans at every step keeps the exponential lower bound. Deciding rarely is what buys the stability.

6. Reinforcement learning hit frequency pathologies years earlier, in sim, at timesteps down to 2000 steps per unit time. [Tallec, Blier, and Ollivier](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.09732) proved the Q-function collapses toward the value function as the timestep shrinks: the action-dependent part scales with the timestep, so approximation error drowns it. [Park, Kim, and Kim](https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.03941) proved a lower bound on policy-gradient variance that scales as 1/δt in stochastic environments (the first author is the same Seohong Park as item 4). [Metelli et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.06836) found that repeating each action for several steps beat acting at full frequency on six of eight benchmarks and tied on the other two; in their one dataset-size sweep, on an FX trading environment, the best repetition length fell as data grew. None of these mechanisms transfers to behavioral cloning as a citation: BC has no Q-function and no score-function gradient. Two of the underlying problems carry over anyway. Each action matters less as the timestep shrinks, and independent per-step noise compounds. RL's fix was called action persistence; ours is called chunking.

7. The third failure lives in the training data. [DAM-VLA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.12105)'s baseline aligned 25Hz camera frames to a 100Hz action stream by repeating each frame four times, then trained a policy to command the 100Hz controller. Identical frames paired with different action labels taught the policy to hesitate: average success fell from 40.95% to 21.9%, with stalling mid-task and jerky motion on the robot. Their fix was architectural (refresh each modality at its own natural rate, cache the visual encoding between refreshes) rather than a different training frequency. [Burns et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.13082) saw the matching shape in offline RL: mixing pendulum data at 50, 100, and 200Hz hurt the finest frequency most, until they rescaled the Q-learning targets so value propagates in physical time rather than per-step time. The mechanisms differ (contradictory labels there, inconsistent Bellman targets here), and the lesson repeats: the data does not contain 100 decisions per second. It came from 25Hz cameras and a human who updates at 2-10Hz.

8. One failure never shows up in sim. [Bronars, Park, and Agrawal](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.02523) trained reaching policies in a system-identified simulator and deployed them zero-shot on a real Franka at 10, 20, 50, and 100Hz. Jitter failures rose from 5.0% at 10Hz to 21.8% at 100Hz, with the cliff between 20 and 50Hz and the failures concentrated in stiff, overdamped controller gains: the policy reacts to transient observations before the joints settle, then amplifies its own reaction into oscillation. Domain randomization did not remove it. The same policies succeeded in simulation at every frequency. Sim hides this cost of fast control, worth remembering when reading sim results, and the flip side works in my favor: sim isolates the statistical costs of fast replanning from the mechanical ones.

9. Two systems papers split the difference on purpose. [PearlVLA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.17924) stretched the chunk from 8 actions to 20, cutting the replanning rate to 40% of baseline, and spent compute refining a latent plan against a frozen world model before decoding; with refinement, the longer chunk cost 1.7 points of success instead of 3.2. [Reactive Diffusion Policy](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.02881) runs a two-speed stack: a latent diffusion planner at 1-2Hz and a sub-millisecond decoder that adjusts the chunk mid-execution from 20-30Hz tactile feedback. Shrinking its chunk from 8 actions to 2 dropped grasp success from 100% to 20%, so the authors kept decisions slow and pushed reactivity into the decoder.

Two conclusions from all of this. First, once inference stops being the constraint, the next constraints are already visible: a policy that resamples independently at every step oscillates between strategies, and the demonstrations contain a few decisions per second because a human made them. Second, every published fix lands in the same place: execute fast, decide at a few Hz, and keep consecutive decisions consistent with each other. Sub-chunks, latent plans, guided resampling, action persistence, and slow-fast stacks are versions of that one design.

Park's post adds a caveat I want to keep visible. He blames test-time distribution shift for all of it, calls it "actually an impossible problem," and expects the pathologies to dissolve once VLAs reach LLM-scale data coverage. If he is right, the constraints above belong to today's data regime rather than to robotics as such. That reading still leaves the experiment below standing, because we deploy in today's regime.

## The experiment

### Design

A frozen [MolmoAct2-LIBERO](https://huggingface.co/allenai/MolmoAct2-LIBERO) checkpoint (5B parameters), evaluated on the ten LIBERO-Long tasks. The model emits a one-second chunk of 10 actions and runs at 10Hz. Nothing gets trained or fine-tuned. The question is whether today's VLA improves when inference becomes free, so the model has to arrive unchanged. LIBERO is also the benchmark Lazzati et al. and PearlVLA report on, which keeps numbers comparable. LIBERO-Long replaces the pick-and-place suite from my first sketch of this design: frontier models sit near 99% on pick-and-place, and a ceiling that tight compresses any effect into noise. If budget allows, a secondary run on the pick-and-place suite (closest to our cube-packing use case) checks that the pattern survives on easier tasks.

**Knob 1, replanning rate.** The policy executes k actions from each chunk before requerying the model, with k swept over {1, 2, 5, 10}. k=10 is deployment today: query once per second, execute the whole chunk. k=1 requeries after every action, which in sim means a 5B model replanning at 10Hz with zero latency. That deletes the inference bottleneck.

**Knob 2, disturbance.** Every configuration runs twice: unmodified, and with temporally correlated (Ornstein-Uhlenbeck) noise added to the executed actions, following Bidirectional Decoding's stochastic protocol. A 40-episode pilot fixes the noise scale so the k=10 baseline lands between 40% and 70% success, off both floor and ceiling; pilot episodes are excluded from analysis.

**Knob 3, the selector.** This arm carries the strong claim, and my first sketch lacked it. At k below 10, the model draws four candidate chunks per query, and a test-time rule keeps the candidate closest to the unexecuted remainder of the previous chunk (Bidirectional Decoding's backward coherence: decay-weighted L2 on the overlapping steps, no retraining, no new information). If per-step replanning fails because each requery samples an independent strategy, this rule repairs it. If per-step replanning fails because the model knows less at the current observation than the old chunk carried forward from a past one, no selector can help.

**Metrics.** Success rate, motion time, and thrash: at each requery, the distance between the new chunk and the remainder of the old one on the steps they both predict. The coherence arm also logs the thrash of the first candidate as a counterfactual, so the selector's effect on plan disagreement is measured inside the same episodes.

### Hypotheses

The statistical null for every comparison is the same: success rates are equal. Three primary comparisons, pre-registered, two-sided Fisher exact tests, Holm-corrected at α = 0.05, 200 episodes per cell (resolves a 14-point gap at power 0.8).

**P1. Deterministic arm, k=1 vs k=10.** The latency-bottleneck worldview predicts k=1 wins or ties: fresh observations at zero cost can only help. I predict k=1 loses by ten points or more. Prior evidence: Bidirectional Decoding's 48.9% vs 64.0%, Park's zero-latency collapse under infinite data, and Zhang et al.'s lower bound for per-step replanning of chunked predictors. Rejecting the null in my direction falsifies the latency worldview on its friendliest terrain.

**P2. Noisy arm, k=1 vs k=10.** I predict the ranking flips and k=1 wins. Reactivity earns its keep once the environment pushes back. This comparison guards against the lazy reading of P1, that replanning is useless; the claim is narrower, that replanning without consistency is useless when nothing is changing.

**P3. Deterministic arm, k=1, coherence selector vs naive.** The decisive comparison. If coherence recovers at least half of the P1 gap, the constraint that surfaced after latency is sampling consistency, and a frozen model fixes it at test time for the cost of three extra samples. If coherence recovers little, the constraint is informational: the demonstrations hold 2-10 decisions per second, the chunk's advantage is what it carries from an earlier observation, and no sampler conjures decisions the data never contained. Lazzati's delayed-policy results and Park's failed history-conditioning both keep this branch live.

What I hope to prove: P1 rejected in my direction, P2 flipped, P3 recovered. That pattern would say the inference bottleneck was never the binding constraint on task success, and it would name the successor (consistency of consecutive decisions) and the constraint waiting behind that one (the decision content of human demonstrations). Either failure would teach more than a clean sweep. P1 surviving the null says frozen VLAs tolerate independent resampling far better than every prior result implies. P3 failing kills the sampling story and moves the bottleneck to the data, which is where Part 2 of this series goes anyway.

**Scope.** Nothing here runs above the model's native 10Hz, and that stays deliberate. The literature covers the regime above 40Hz; this experiment tests the claim underneath it, that replanning speed binds at all. If the deterministic arm shows no gain at k=1, a faster clock changes nothing. Retraining at higher frequencies on upsampled data stays out of scope; DAM-VLA ran that experiment, and a frozen model already carries whatever dose of the label-collision problem its training data had.
